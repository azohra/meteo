---
title: Tune the wire
description: "Read the transport report every build prints, and let it decide what to change: connections, hosts, or nothing."
---

From engine 0.1.3, every `meteo forecast build` prints a `[wire]` block
beside its summary line. It is the evidence for every transport decision an
operator faces — whether to raise a fetch gate, move a model to a different
host, or leave a slow-looking lane exactly as it is.

```text
Published 4 profiles for 2026-08-14T12:00:00Z (4129 downloads, 8219 MiB).
[wire] 4135 requests (0 failed), 8223.4 MiB, wall 113.6 s
[wire] wire-busy 113.1 s (100% of wall) · mean concurrency 4.9 · busy throughput 72.7 MiB/s
[wire] request latency p50 91 ms · p90 303 ms · max 2.7 s · mean size 2.0 MiB
[wire] cpu user 185.5 s · system 16.6 s
[wire]   hpfx.collab.science.gc.ca: 4130 requests, 8222.8 MiB, mean 134 ms
```

Line by line:

- **requests / failed / MiB / wall** — the build's whole transport story in
  one line. Failures here are attempts the engine retried or gave up on;
  a persistent non-zero count is a host telling you something.
- **wire-busy** — the union of time when at least one request was in
  flight. Busy ≈ wall means the build never waited on anything *but* the
  wire.
- **mean concurrency** — in-flight request time divided by busy time. Read
  it against the builder's fetch gate: pinned at the gate means the gate is
  the constraint; stuck at 1.0 means something upstream serializes.
- **busy throughput** — bytes over busy time. This is the host's delivered
  rate at your concurrency, the number to compare across hosts.
- **latency p50/p90/max · mean size** — high p50 against small mean sizes
  means round trips dominate and concurrency is the lever; low p50 with low
  throughput means the pipe itself is the ceiling.
- **cpu user/system** — decode and derivation cost. On a two-core runner,
  user time approaching twice the wall means compute, not transport, paces
  the build.
- **per-host rows** — one model can touch several hosts; the row tells you
  which one is slow, failing, or both.

## Three verdicts

| The report shows | The build is | The lever |
| --- | --- | --- |
| busy ≈ wall, concurrency pinned at the gate, low throughput | wire-bound | a faster host, or fewer bytes — not more connections |
| high p50 against small mean sizes, throughput far below the host's known rate | round-trip-bound | more connections, within the provider's budget |
| cpu ≈ cores × wall while busy falls | compute-bound | the wire is no longer the story; look at decode |

A fourth reading matters as much: **all three in balance** — busy near wall,
cpu high, gate saturated. That build is already overlapping fetch and decode
well. Leave it alone.

## The two ECCC hosts are not equal

ECCC publishes the same dated tree on `dd.weather.gc.ca` and on the
sanctioned high-bandwidth mirror `hpfx.collab.science.gc.ca`
(`METEO_DATAMART_BASE` selects it). They are not interchangeable in
practice: two days of reports from one operator's GitHub-hosted runners
measured dd at 10–16 MiB/s per fresh build — with one ensemble build
failing a tenth of its requests — while hpfx delivered 73 MiB/s with zero
failures at the same five-connection gate.

The doctrine that follows: **balance lanes by measured transfer time, not
by bytes.** A byte-balanced split can still leave one lane running seven
times longer than the other. Put the heavy streams on the mirror, keep
dd's lane light, and re-read the reports after any move — these are one
operator's numbers from one vantage; measure your own before copying them.

The connection budgets themselves are engine constants, sized against
MSC's documented per-day request budget (five per Datamart host) and NOAA's
S3 buckets (ten per bucket; fourteen for NAM, where ~1 s per ranged GET
makes round trips the constraint). The report tells you when a gate is
saturated; the provider's budget tells you whether it may widen.

## The reference operator

The pipeline that publishes real forecasts for acrophobia.ca is public:
[`azohra/acrophobia-forecasts`](https://github.com/azohra/acrophobia-forecasts).
Its workflow is one worked answer to every question on this page — lanes
grouped by provider host, heavy streams on the mirror, per-model uploads
that survive a timeout, an engine pin that refuses to run anything
unpinned, and the scheduling rules from
[Schedule builds](/docs/publish/schedule-builds/) applied end to end. Read
`.github/workflows/build-data.yml` first; the comments carry the
reasoning, and its Actions log carries live `[wire]` blocks from every
tick.

One ordering rule its history teaches: **publish before you pin.** A
lockfile cannot resolve an engine version that is not on the registry yet —
release the engine first, then bump the pin and refresh the lockfile in
the same commit, or the next tick fails at install.
